1. Respiratory Structures & Pressure Gradients

How Gases Move: The Pressure Gradient

Gases always move from areas of high pressure to areas of low pressure. Your respiratory system uses this rule to move air:

  • Conducting Airways: The nose, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, and bronchioles transport air, filter it, and warm it. (No gas exchange happens here).
  • Gas Exchange: The alveoli (tiny air sacs) are the only structures where oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse into and out of the blood.
  • Mechanical Structures: The diaphragm (a dome-shaped muscle at the base of the chest) and the external intercostal muscles (between the ribs) drive ventilation.

Inhalation: When the diaphragm contracts (flattens) and the intercostals contract (lifting the rib cage), the thoracic cavity expands. This increases lung volume, dropping alveolar pressure below atmospheric pressure (PATM). Air rushes in.

Exhalation: When these muscles relax, the thoracic cavity shrinks. The volume decreases, causing alveolar pressure to rise above atmospheric pressure. Air is pushed out.
